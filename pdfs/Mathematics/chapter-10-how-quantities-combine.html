CHAPTER 10 · DATA & QUANTITIES

How Quantities Combine:
Understanding Data

When different groups or quantities are combined, their size and importance matter. This chapter develops weighted averages and shows how charts can help us visualise and interpret data.

10.1 Combining Things

TOPIC 10.1

Combining Things

When quantities from different groups are combined, we cannot always take their ordinary average directly. We have to consider the size or importance of each group.

Main idea: This leads to the idea of weighted averages.
TOPIC 10.1.1

Average of Averages

Suppose two or more groups have different numbers of objects or people. Their averages cannot simply be averaged unless the groups are of equal size.

For two groups:

  • First group: average = \(a\), number of items = \(n\)
  • Second group: average = \(b\), number of items = \(m\)
\[ \boxed{\text{Combined Average}=\frac{an+bm}{n+m}} \]

Here, the number of items in each group acts as its weight.

Important: If the two groups have the same number of items, we can simply take the average of their averages.
\[ \boxed{\text{Combined Average}=\frac{a+b}{2}} \]

For three groups:

\[ \boxed{\text{Combined Average}=\frac{ap+bq+cr}{p+q+r}} \]

where \(p,q,r\) are the sizes of the three groups.

TOPIC 10.1.2

Mixtures

When two or more quantities with different concentrations are mixed, the concentration of the mixture depends on the amount of each substance.

For example, if two solutions have different concentrations, the final concentration cannot usually be found by simply taking their ordinary average. The amount of each solution acts as its weight.

\[ \boxed{ \text{Weighted Mean} = \frac{w_1x_1+w_2x_2+\cdots+w_nx_n} {w_1+w_2+\cdots+w_n} } \]

where \(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n\) are the values and \(w_1,w_2,\ldots,w_n\) are their corresponding weights.

TOPIC 10.1.3

Weighted Average

A weighted average is an average in which different values are given different importance or weights.

The value having a larger weight contributes more to the final average.

\[ \boxed{ \text{Weighted Average} = \frac{\sum(\text{Value}\times\text{Weight})} {\sum\text{Weights}} } \]

In simple words: Weighted Average = Total of (Value × Weight) ÷ Total Weight.

Worked example

If marks \(60\%\), \(64\%\), and \(73\%\) have weights in the ratio \(3:2:5\), then:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Weighted Average} &=\frac{60\times3+64\times2+73\times5}{3+2+5}\\[4pt] &=\frac{180+128+365}{10}\\[4pt] &=67.3\% \end{aligned} \]

Therefore, the weighted average is \(67.3\%\).

TOPIC 10.1.4

Custom Weights

Sometimes different quantities are assigned weights according to their relative importance.

Ratios

Weights may be given as ratios, such as \(3:2:5\).

Percentages

Weights may be expressed using percentages.

Importance

Weights may simply be numbers representing relative importance.

Important: The weights do not necessarily have to add up to \(100\). We divide by the total of all the weights.

A larger weight means that the corresponding value has a greater effect on the final answer.

TOPIC 10.1.5

Applications of Weighted Average

Weighted averages are used when different parts contribute differently to the final result.

Marks & grades

Different assessments may have different weights.

Product ratings

Different groups or criteria can contribute differently.

Hotel & restaurant ratings

Ratings can be combined while accounting for their weights.

Combining averages

Group sizes determine how much each average contributes.

Mixtures

The amount of each substance affects the final result.

Different importance

Use weights when quantities do not contribute equally.

Main idea: Use a weighted average when the final result should depend on how much, how long, or how important each part is.

10.2 Visualising and Interpreting Data

TOPIC 10.2

Visualising and Interpreting Data

Data can be represented using different types of charts and graphs. These visual representations help us compare quantities and identify patterns or trends.

In this chapter, we study stacked columns and 100% stacked bar charts.

TOPIC 10.2.2

100% Stacked Bar Chart

A 100% stacked bar chart shows each group as a whole equal to \(100\%\). The individual sections show the percentage contribution of different components.

Example

If a group consists of:

  • \(40\%\) boys
  • \(60\%\) girls

the complete bar represents \(100\%\), while the two sections represent \(40\%\) and \(60\%\).

TOPIC 10.2.3

Difference Between Stacked and 100% Stacked Charts

Stacked Chart

Shows the actual total as well as its components. It is useful when we want to compare total quantities.

100% Stacked Chart

Every bar has the same total of \(100\%\). It shows the proportion or percentage of each component.

Important: A 100% stacked chart cannot tell us the actual total size of each group. It only tells us how the total is divided.
TOPIC 10.2.4

Interpreting Data

Charts can help us draw conclusions from data. While interpreting a chart, we should look at several features.

Total quantity

Look at the overall total.

Individual components

Identify the parts that make up the total.

Percentages

Compare the percentage or fraction of each component.

Differences

Compare the groups and their components.

Trends

Look for patterns or trends in the data.

Remember: Sometimes an overall average may hide important differences between groups. Dividing data into smaller groups can help us understand these differences better.

Key Points of Chapter 10

  • An average of averages cannot always be found by simply averaging the averages.
  • The size of a group can act as its weight.
  • A weighted average gives different values different importance.
  • The basic weighted-average formula is \[ \boxed{ \text{Weighted Average} = \frac{\sum(\text{Value}\times\text{Weight})} {\sum\text{Weights}} } \]
  • In mixtures, the amount of each substance affects the final result.
  • Custom weights represent the relative importance of different values.
  • Stacked charts show totals and their components.
  • 100% stacked charts show the percentage contribution of each component.
  • A 100% stacked chart compares proportions but does not show actual totals.
  • Data should be interpreted carefully to identify patterns and trends.
When quantities combine, consider not only their values, but also their size or importance.

Main Idea of the Chapter

When quantities combine, we must consider not only their values but also their size or importance. This is why weighted averages are useful. Data can then be represented visually through charts to make comparisons and patterns easier to understand.
